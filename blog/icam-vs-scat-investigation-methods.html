ICAM vs SCAT: Which Incident Investigation Method Should You Use?

What is ICAM?

ICAM (Incident Cause Analysis Method) is a systematic investigation methodology developed in Australia by the Minerals Council. It's designed to identify the organisational factors that contribute to incidents, not just the immediate causes.

ICAM is built on James Reason's "Swiss Cheese" model of accident causation. It recognises that incidents rarely have a single cause. Instead, multiple failures must align, like holes in slices of Swiss cheese, for an incident to occur.

The ICAM Framework

ICAM uses a structured approach with five key elements:

  1. Absent or Failed Defences: The barriers that should have prevented the incident
  2. Individual/Team Actions: The actions (or inactions) that directly led to the incident
  3. Task/Environmental Conditions: The workplace conditions present at the time
  4. Organisational Factors: The systemic issues within the organisation (using PEEPO categories)
  5. Risk Controls: Recommendations to prevent recurrence

PEEPO Categories

ICAM uses PEEPO to categorize organisational factors: People, Environment, Equipment, Procedures, and Organization. This ensures investigators look beyond individual blame to systemic issues.

What is SCAT?

SCAT (Systematic Cause Analysis Technique) was developed by the International Loss Control Institute (ILCI) as part of the Bird-Germain loss causation model. It's one of the most widely used investigation methods globally.

SCAT uses a domino-effect model: incidents happen when a sequence of causes align, like falling dominoes. Remove one domino, and you break the chain.

The SCAT Framework

SCAT investigates incidents through four levels:

  1. Loss: The injury, damage, or business interruption
  2. Incident: The contact with energy or substance that caused the loss
  3. Immediate Causes: Substandard acts and conditions
  4. Basic Causes: Personal factors and job factors
  5. Lack of Control: Inadequate program, standards, or compliance

SCAT provides extensive checklists for each level, making it easier for investigators to identify contributing factors without missing anything.

Key Differences Between ICAM and SCAT

1. Philosophy

ICAM is explicitly "blame-free." It focuses on systemic failures, not individual fault. The methodology assumes that people generally try to do the right thing, and incidents occur due to organisational weaknesses.

SCAT identifies "substandard acts" as immediate causes, which can feel more blame-oriented. However, it does trace these back to system failures.

2. Structure

ICAM is more flexible. Investigators build a narrative and identify contributing factors organically, using the framework as a guide.

SCAT is more prescriptive. It provides detailed checklists with hundreds of potential causes. This is helpful for consistency but can feel mechanical.

3. Organisational Focus

ICAM puts heavy emphasis on organisational factors through PEEPO. It's designed to surface management system failures.

SCAT addresses organisational issues through "Lack of Control" but with less structured guidance.

When to Use Each Method

Choose ICAM When:

  • Investigating serious or complex incidents
  • Your organisation wants to move away from blame culture
  • You need to identify systemic/management failures
  • Operating in high-risk industries (mining, construction, rail)
  • Regulatory requirements favor human factors analysis
  • You want investigations that drive organisational change

Choose SCAT When:

  • Investigating high volumes of incidents consistently
  • Your team has limited investigation experience
  • You need standardized, comparable data across sites
  • Quick turnaround is important
  • Your industry already uses SCAT widely (easier benchmarking)
  • You want checklist-driven thoroughness

Can You Combine ICAM and SCAT?

Absolutely. Many organisations use a tiered approach:

  • Minor incidents: Use SCAT checklists for quick, consistent analysis
  • Serious incidents: Use full ICAM methodology with PEEPO organisational analysis
  • High-potential incidents: Use ICAM regardless of actual severity

You can also use SCAT's detailed checklists as a prompt when conducting ICAM investigations. The two methods complement each other well.

ICAM in Practice: High-Hazard Industries

ICAM has become the dominant methodology in several high-risk sectors, particularly in Australia, Africa, and the Middle East. Understanding why helps clarify when it's the right choice over SCAT.

Mining

Mining operations in Australia, South Africa, and West Africa have widely adopted ICAM following fatality investigations where surface-level analysis repeatedly failed to identify management system failures. Regulators in these regions increasingly expect ICAM-aligned reports for high-consequence events. In underground and open-cut mining, where incidents often involve multiple overlapping hazards, ICAM's organisational factor analysis is particularly valuable, it surfaces the gap between work-as-imagined (in the procedure) and work-as-done (in practice).

Construction and Civil Works

On large infrastructure and construction projects with multiple contractors and subcontractors, ICAM's PEEPO framework is especially effective. Many construction fatalities involve organisational factors, inadequate supervision, compressed timelines, poor change management, that SCAT's checklist approach can underweight. The blame-free framing of ICAM also tends to generate more honest witness interviews on construction sites where workers fear repercussions.

Oil, Gas, and Process Industries

Process safety incidents typically involve complex systems with multiple simultaneous failures. Both ICAM and SCAT are used, often alongside Bow-Tie analysis or Fault Tree Analysis. SCAT's prescriptive checklists suit the standardization needs of multinational operators; ICAM's depth suits major accident investigations. Many companies operate a tiered system: SCAT for recordable incidents and near-misses, ICAM for high-potential events and LTIs.

Conclusion

Both ICAM and SCAT are effective incident investigation methodologies. The right choice depends on your organisation's culture, industry, incident severity, and investigation resources.

Our recommendation: For organisations committed to learning from incidents and driving systemic improvement, ICAM's blame-free approach and organisational focus typically delivers better long-term results. SCAT's checklists can supplement ICAM investigations for thoroughness.

Practical Tips for Switching from SCAT to ICAM

Many organisations that have used SCAT for years face challenges when transitioning to ICAM. Here are the most common friction points and how to address them:

  • Start with facilitated ICAM workshops. ICAM requires a mindset shift, investigators need to understand why organisational factors matter, not just how to fill in the template. A 2-day workshop with real case studies makes a significant difference to early quality.
  • Run parallel investigations initially. For the first 3-6 months, run SCAT and ICAM simultaneously on serious incidents. This builds confidence and lets your team compare outputs before fully committing.
  • Don't abandon SCAT for minor events. SCAT's checklist efficiency is genuinely useful for high-volume, low-severity investigations. A tiered approach keeps ICAM for where it adds most value.
  • Involve line managers early. ICAM investigations often surface organisational factors that reflect management system weaknesses. Involving managers in the investigation process, not just receiving the report, dramatically improves implementation of recommendations.
